1 · Greenlight MY PICK

"Greenlight me a film…". You pitch, we say yes. The one word in the business that means "this one gets made", and it turns into "Greenlit for you" over the results.

2 · Fade in

"Fade in: a slow-burn 90s thriller". Every screenplay opens with FADE IN. Your sentence becomes the first line. Beautiful in the sheet, softer as a button.

3 · Treatment

"Write a treatment…". A treatment is literally a short description of a film. The most exact; the word also means medical care to most people.

Recommendation

Final pick: Greenlight, with the new Signal mark (a three-light signal, bottom light blue). It is the most famous industry verb that a non-insider knows, it reads as a request ("Greenlight me a film…"), it counts well ("3 free Greenlights left"), and the past tense gives the results a title nobody else has ("Greenlit for you"). Ten letters still fits beside search on a 375pt phone. Runner-up: Fade in if you want it to feel like writing, not approving; best inside the sheet, weaker as a button verb. Third: Treatment, the most literal, held back by its medical meaning. Avoid: Double bill and Feature (Double Feature is already a content format), Take (clashes with your take on a film), Action (a genre), Showtime and Zoom (brand names), Channel and Now showing (read as live TV or cinema listings, and Tonight already owns "what's on"), Rough cut (a cousin of Montage and Recut, and "Rough cut me" doesn't work), Mise-en-scène (nobody can spell it on a button).
